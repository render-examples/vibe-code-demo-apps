Patch Tips & Guides

Grab your favorite mono synth (or your DAW's soft-synth of choice) and follow along. We explain the jargon as we go, so newcomers won't get lost in acronym soup.

Building a Classic Analog Bass Patch

That fat, growling bass on every Minimoog record isn't magic — it's a formula. Here's the recipe:

  1. Oscillators: Stack two VCOs (voltage-controlled oscillators). Set one to a sawtooth wave, detune the second slightly (5–10 cents) for thickness.
  2. Sub oscillator: If your synth has one, add it an octave down for low-end weight.
  3. Filter: Route into a low-pass filter (that ladder filter warmth is the secret sauce). Cutoff around 30–40%, resonance at 20–30%.
  4. Filter envelope: Fast attack, medium decay, low sustain, short release — a quick "pluck" that opens the filter on note-on and settles fast.
  5. Amp envelope: Fast attack, no sustain dip, medium release so notes don't click off.
  6. Glide: Add a touch of portamento for that classic sliding bass line feel between notes.

Play it an octave lower than feels natural — analog bass patches almost always sit lower in the mix than you expect.

West Coast Synthesis 101: FM Meets Wavefolding

"West coast" synthesis (named for the Bay Area lineage of Don Buchla's designs) skips the standard subtractive filter approach in favor of FM and wavefolding for harmonic complexity.

  1. Start with a sine wave oscillator instead of a sawtooth — west coast patches build complexity additively rather than carving it away with filters.
  2. FM (frequency modulation): Use a second oscillator to modulate the pitch of the first at audio rate. Small amounts add metallic edge; large amounts create bell tones and noise-like textures.
  3. Wavefolder: Route your sine through a wavefolder to fold the waveform back on itself, generating rich odd harmonics without any filter at all.
  4. Envelope the fold amount: Modulate the wavefolder's fold depth with an envelope for a "blooming" harmonic swell on each note.
  5. Low-pass gate: If your instrument has one (a hallmark of Buchla-style design), use it instead of a standard VCA + filter combo — it shapes both amplitude and tone together, like a mute on a plucked string.

The Make Noise 0-Coast is purpose-built for exactly this kind of patching — start there if you want to try it hands-on.

Getting Lush Pads from Any Poly Synth

Pads are about space and movement, not precision. Here's how to get that Juno-106-style wash on nearly any polyphonic synth:

  1. Detune two or three oscillators against each other slightly — this beating between close pitches is what creates motion.
  2. Slow attack, slow release on your amp envelope — think 1–3 seconds on each so notes fade in and out instead of snapping.
  3. Filter it dark: Low-pass filter cutoff around 40%, with a slow LFO gently sweeping it up and down for evolving brightness.
  4. Chorus or ensemble effect: If your synth has built-in chorus (the Juno-106's is legendary), turn it on. If not, add one after — it thickens multiple voices into one lush wash.
  5. Reverb, generously: Pads live in the reverb tail. A long hall or plate reverb glues everything together.

Play slow-moving chords and let the patch do the work — pads are about patience, not performance.
